The Initiative Scorecard: Nine Rejections, Zero Ballots

From Paoniapedia, the free encyclopedia of the Town of Paonia
The Initiative Scorecard: Nine Rejections, Zero Ballots
Analysis
Record
TypeAnalysis
First recorded2026-08-01
Topicsinitiatives ballot petition-rights clerk buchner accountability scorecard

Between May 2025 and June 2026, Paonia residents submitted nine citizen initiative petitions to Town Hall — on short-term rentals, on surveillance cameras, on autonomous robots. All nine were rejected by Town Clerk Samira Vetter. None reached signature collection. None reached the ballot. The rejection rate for citizen lawmaking in Paonia stands at one hundred percent.

Nine letters. Zero citations to any Colorado court decision. Zero applications of the three-part test Colorado courts actually use to distinguish legislative from administrative measures.

One conclusion, five labels

Across the five rejections of the surveillance initiative alone, the Clerk's office reached the same result under five different formulations: "administrative function" (April 3), "purely executive acts" (April 13), "managerial and operational function" (April 27), "administrative and executive functions" (May 28), and "administrative, managerial, and operational in nature" (June 9). The label rotated with each letter. The outcome never did.

The experiment that proved the result was fixed

The May 28, 2026 letter rested on specific language in the petition — its references to the Town's power to "authorize, fund, and contract" and to "budget appropriations." So the petitioners ran an experiment: they removed every provision the letter had quoted. The resubmitted measure was narrowed to a bare declaration of public policy — that the Town shall not conduct, cause, or permit continuous electronic surveillance of persons in public spaces.

The June 9 letter rejected that too — this time by quoting the replacement language ("conduct, cause, or permit") back as itself administrative. Whatever words the measure uses become the words that condemn it. A legal review whose outcome does not depend on the text under review is not a review. It is a result.

The office knows how to do it properly

The first rejection on the ledger is the tell that the other eight are different in kind. In May 2025, the Clerk rejected the first short-term-rental petition for a genuine form defect — the statute's two-designated-representatives requirement — citing the exact statutory subsection, and the petitioners corrected it within days. That is what clerk review under C.R.S. § 31-11-106(1) actually is: a narrow, ministerial check of the petition's form.

Every rejection after that one was something else — a judgment that the subject of the measure was off-limits to voters. That judgment belongs to courts, not clerks. The statute's five-business-day review window exists precisely because the legislature designed clerk review to be quick, mechanical, and shallow. A review deep enough to adjudicate constitutional doctrine is a review the statute never authorized.

The attorney in the room

The May 28, 2025 letter states the rejection came "after research and discussion with the Town Attorney." Town Attorney Clayton Buchner's involvement is documented from the first substantive rejection onward — including a legal memorandum analyzing the STR initiative that existed at the time of rejection but was withheld from the petitioners for nearly ten months. The rejections issue over the Clerk's signature; the doctrine issues from the attorney's office.

The tells

  • The April 27 letter misidentified the measure it was rejecting — its first sentence names the "Paonia Short-Term Lodging Policy" while rejecting the Surveillance Policy Ordinance. A case-by-case legal judgment does not paste the wrong petition's name into its opening line. A template does. source
  • That same letter was dated April 27 — the last day of the statutory five-business-day deadline — but was not transmitted until April 28, after the petitioners sent a written demand invoking the deadline.
  • Each successive letter's theory grew broader. By June 9, the office's position was that the entire "subject of surveillance in public spaces … involves the administration of Town property, public facilities, public safety resources, contracts, and Town operations." Under that formulation, virtually no citizen initiative on any subject could survive — nearly every ordinance touches operations, property, or public safety. Including the ones the Board itself passes.

Why it matters

The initiative power exists so that citizens can legislate around their elected board. Colorado's constitution reserves it to the people; the statute gives the clerk only a form check. When the same office rejects nine petitions in thirteen months under a rotating vocabulary, without ever citing a case, the right hasn't been regulated — it has been repealed locally, one letter at a time. The question is no longer whether any single rejection was correct. It is whether a citizen of Paonia can place any question on the ballot at all.
